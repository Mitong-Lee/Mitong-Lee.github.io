## Fight for Authenticity: To what extent is the revival of film photography a cultural response to the growing influence of artificial intelligence in everyday life?

In the post-pandemic era, the use of artificial intelligence in people’s daily lives has become increasingly widespread. AI is not only capable of creating images and recommending products or films, but also provides assistance across a wide range of fields through smart assistants. Whilst AI brings numerous conveniences and offers boundless potential for future applications, it has simultaneously exacerbated people’s detachment from the real world. As the line between AI-generated content and human-created content becomes increasingly blurred, and digital experiences become ever more widespread, many people are seeking ways to rebuild the connection with the world around them that has been lost. In a conversation with my mother, I once remarked, ‘If your generation (my mother’s) was the one that learned to distinguish between the internet and reality, ours is the generation that must learn to distinguish between AI and the real world.’  It is against this backdrop that film photography has experienced an unexpected revival, and fortunately, I am part of that revival. This has given me sufficient insight into this trend to write this article. Unlike digital photography, which can be mastered quickly with almost no learning curve and is virtually foolproof, film photography demands physical engagement and long-term learning; it requires you to tolerate uncertainty and high costs, and it demands patience. Consequently, the return to film photography is not merely a nostalgic longing for a bygone era when everything was fundamentally different—when people possessed a ‘sense of being alive’  and enjoyed life rather than merely pursuing efficiency—but also a reaction to the cultural shifts brought about by our growing reliance on artificial intelligence.

  

The fact that film photography allows for the reconnection with the tactile aspect of experience is one of the reasons why it is becoming increasingly popular. The advancement of artificial intelligence has made our lives ever more digitalized. Pictures, conversations, songs, and even artworks can be created without the need for any kind of physical presence –  they emerge exclusively from the workings of algorithms. This trend has caused more and more individuals to become interested in doing something in order to regain connection with the tangible world. Photography using film satisfies that craving perfectly because, unlike digital photos that exist only as electronic documents on one's computer or phone, the emergence of film photographs involves several stages at which the use of physical things becomes indispensable.

The same longing for materiality is apparent even among those using other analog technologies. As mentioned by Porter (2022), many people are becoming more interested in analog activities since they offer a higher level of tactile involvement and an emotionally closer experience compared to their digital counterparts. Likewise, as discussed by Grossman (2017), young photographers become fascinated with film cameras precisely because such a medium offers a more honest and intentional experience compared to digital photography. In contrast to the latter, which promotes photographing numerous photos that will probably never be looked at again, film photography compels one to think deeply about every photo taken.

  

In this regard, the advent of film photography is strongly associated with its inherent ambiguity and flaws in opposition to the rise of artificial intelligence in visual arts. Nowadays, AI can be used to produce extremely realistic images, mimicking the results that would be obtained by real cameras. By doing so, AI puts into question the established concept that the photograph presents the true reality. The more advanced AI technologies become, the more blurred the line between the real and virtual becomes, creating uncertainty about the reality depicted in pictures.

Under such circumstances, film photography may be considered a type of visual reassurance since it is directly linked to physical reality. In contrast to AI-generated images, which have no physical connection to reality since they are created using computations, film photography involves light exposure, chemical processing, and negatives. Therefore, film photography maintains direct contact between image and physical reality. Moreover, film photography incorporates imperfections such as grain, blurring, and light leaks, differentiating it from flawless and frequently enhanced digital and AI-generated images.

As Tidy (2022) argues, younger photographers prefer film specifically because of its unpredictability and inability to produce the perfectly edited photo. In a similar way, Grossman (2017) emphasizes that imperfections are more and more perceived as proof of authenticity rather than a sign of poor quality. Significantly, studies on synthetic media reveal that the use of AI-generated content can undermine public confidence in visual images and make it harder to distinguish fiction from reality (Vaccari & Chadwick, 2020). Under these conditions, film photography gains importance not only as an aesthetic preference but as an alternative to the increasing visual instability.

  

There is another reason why film photography is becoming increasingly popular once again: it offers a counterbalance to the culture of instant gratification and excessive positive reinforcement fuelled by artificial intelligence. The former refers to a psychological state and consumer trend characterised by immediately yielding to one’s emotional, physiological or material needs in pursuit of ‘instant gratification’, whilst the latter refers to a situation where excessive external positive reinforcement actually diminishes one’s original motivation for doing something. The primary goal of technology—particularly artificial intelligence—is efficiency, and anything that requires time runs counter to this objective. This means that whenever people ask AI to perform a task—such as generating images, answering questions, editing photos or recommending entertainment content—they receive instant gratification.

Photographers, on the other hand, need a certain amount of patience, as they cannot see the results of their photographs immediately—the film still needs to be developed, a process that can take days or even weeks. As Coles (2024) explains, digital cameras often provide instant feedback, helping photographers to assess the quality of their work immediately. However, film cameras do not provide this instant feedback; conversely, this fosters patience during the shooting process. Film cameras also reduce so-called external positive reinforcement—at least during the act of shooting itself—allowing one to better maintain the enthusiasm derived from the positive reinforcement of photography itself, thereby enhancing sustainability. Bolton (2025) elaborates on how, in today’s digital world where everything pursues efficiency, film photography has become a silent rebellion against speed.

  

In summary, the resurgence of film photography is not purely a matter of nostalgia, but rather a reaction—or, one might say, a fear—of the growing influence of artificial intelligence in our lives. The fact that artificial intelligence has reduced the reliance on human intervention in most tasks, the increasingly blurred boundaries between reality and simulated environments, and the prevalence of a culture of instant gratification have left people feeling replaced, insignificant and lacking control over their own lives. These are the primary reasons why people have begun to seek authentic human experiences. This is because the process involved in film photography not only imbues individuals with a sense of authenticity and agency, but also allows them to create images through patience. In fact, as can be seen from the above discussion, although film photography appears to exist within the age of artificial intelligence, this technology still embodies a human longing.
