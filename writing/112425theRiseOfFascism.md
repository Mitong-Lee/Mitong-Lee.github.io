## THE RISE OF FASCISM

Post-war Europe was clearly an era fraught with crisis and turmoil. The First World War not only destroyed the old international order but also failed (as the American idealist Wilson had hoped) to establish a new one: economic collapse, widespread unemployment, political polarisation and widespread disillusionment with democratic institutions plunged the entire continent into a state of confusion and unease. Against this backdrop, Fascism and Communism emerged as two extreme political ideologies, ultimately profoundly altering the course of European and indeed world history. The focus of this article, regrettably, is not the Russian Communist movement, which began in 1917 and came to an end in 1991, but rather the former: Fascism.

The very first fascist in the history was arguably Niccolò Machiavelli, who, in The Prince, described his own political philosophy: an ideology that rejected secular morality and relied solely on the monopoly of violence to maintain rule. The second fascist was the poet Gabriele D’Annunzio, whose dictatorial corporatist rule in Fiume City (and, of course, the establishment of the so-called ‘Black Shirts’) provided an excellent model for Mussolini—the third fascist.

The first official fascist party was founded in Milan, Italy, by the newspaper editor Benito Mussolini (a cynical, romantic, bald man); three years after its establishment, it successfully seized power in Italy and subsequently influenced the development of the Nazi Party in Germany.

At the same time, the Nazi Party, led by Adolf Hitler, exploited Germany’s post-war social crisis and nationalist sentiment to gradually seize power, pushing fascism in a more radical and extreme direction.

Although fascist regimes exhibited distinct characteristics in different countries, they generally shared traits such as anti-liberalism, the cult of personality, the nationalisation of political parties, the militarisation of the military, and the militarisation of the state.

This paper will begin by examining the European situation following the First World War to analyse the historical context of the rise of fascism; it will then discuss the rise of Mussolini and the Italian Fascist movement, as well as that of Hitler and the German Nazi Party; finally, it will summarise the main characteristics of fascist rule, thereby exploring why fascism was able to expand rapidly across post-war Europe and exert such a profound influence on 20th-century history.

  

### EUROPE AFTER THE GREAT WAR

> “a mutilated victory” — Gabriele D’Annunzio


Following the end of the First World War, almost all of Europe’s major powers suffered to varying degrees, ranging from economic collapse and population decline to being effectively wiped off the map.

Although the United Kingdom was one of the victorious nations, it had paid a heavy price during the war. The loss of a vast number of young lives fostered a widespread aversion to war within society. At the same time, the British economy was mired in debt due to the prolonged conflict (the United States, should you be curious as to who held these debts). Its international standing also began to be challenged by the United States, whilst Germany posed a threat to Britain’s policy of continental balance of power for the first time. Although the British government successfully maintained the parliamentary democratic system in the aftermath, doubts regarding its governance continued to arise.

France achieved a hard-won victory, yet vast swathes of industrial areas and farmland in the north were devastated by waves of heavy German artillery fire; the extent of the destruction to the ground was so severe that even the German forces themselves found it difficult to advance across French territory. With over a million deaths nationwide, virtually everyone in the country became a pacifist. France remained long shrouded in fear of a resurgent Germany; consequently, the French government imposed almost brutal measures on Germany in the Treaty of Versailles, hoping to ensure its long-term security through reparations and military restrictions. However, persistent security anxieties and economic pressures also led to French politics becoming increasingly polarised.

Germany, meanwhile, became one of the most unstable nations in the post-war period. The former German Empire collapsed outright in 1918, with Emperor Wilhelm II fleeing to the Netherlands, after which a new democratic German state was established (known as the Weimar Republic). However, the new government faced immense pressure from the very outset. The Treaty of Versailles required Germany to accept responsibility for the war, pay massive reparations, reduce its military, and cede territory. This ‘humiliating peace’ stirred widespread patriotic sentiment. Several near-bankruptcies in the 1920s and the Great Depression of 1929 further eroded German society’s trust in the democratic system. It also provided grounds for belief in the so-called ‘stab-in-the-back’ theory—which held that German soldiers had been on the verge of victory in 1917 (it is worth noting that Germany’s homeland remained uninvaded until its defeat, whilst northern France was occupied), and that the final defeat was solely due to Jewish treachery.

The United States emerged as one of the greatest economic beneficiaries of the war. European nations accumulated substantial debts to the United States, enabling America to gradually supplant Britain as the world’s financial centre. In the 1920s, the US economy experienced rapid growth, a period known as the ‘Roaring Twenties’. However, isolationist tendencies persisted within the United States, with a reluctance to become overly involved in European affairs. Following the Wall Street crash of 1929, the US economic crisis swiftly spread across the globe, further exacerbating the social and political crises in European nations.

Austria-Hungary, meanwhile, disintegrated completely in the aftermath of the war. This dual monarchy, composed of multiple ethnic groups, split following its final struggles into the German-Austrian Republic, the Hungarian Soviet Republic (a short-lived socialist state, though, once again, the subject of this article is not socialism), the First Czechoslovak Republic, the Second Polish Republic, the Kingdom of Yugoslavia, the Kingdom of Romania (which gained Transylvania), and the Kingdom of Italy (which gained the Istrian Peninsula). In April 1919, Vorarlberg, Austria’s westernmost province, held a referendum, with a majority voting to ‘join Switzerland’. However, Switzerland ignored the result.

Russia’s losses during the war were equally catastrophic. The prolonged conflict led to economic collapse, shortages of supplies and social unrest, ultimately triggering the Russian Revolution of 1917. Following the October Revolution, the Bolsheviks established a Soviet regime, and a civil war subsequently broke out, in which they emerged victorious against the Republican White Army, the monarchists and local nationalist regimes. The emergence of the Soviet Union caused widespread concern amongst conservative forces and the middle classes across Europe regarding the spread of communist revolution; consequently, many began to support fascist movements with a distinct anti-communist stance.

Overall, although the war had ended in post-World War I Europe, true stability had not been restored. Economic crises, ethnic tensions, political polarisation and fear of communism left European society in a state of widespread unease. Against this backdrop, fascism gradually came to be seen by many as a ‘necessary solution’ to communism and the failure of democratic systems; some politicians also hoped to use the fascist movement to seize power, only to discard it afterwards (one example being the Finnish President, Pehr Evind Svinhufvud). In any case, the fascist movement ultimately seized power in Italy and Germany.

  

### BENITO MUSSOLINI AND LA FASCISMO IN ITALY

> “In Italy, fascists divide themselves into two categories: fascists and anti-fascists.” 
> — Ennio Flaiano, Italian writer

Following the end of the First World War, Italian society was plunged into a severe political and economic crisis. Although Italy was among the victorious powers, many felt that the Paris Peace Conference had not granted the country the territorial and international status it deserved. Amidst this atmosphere of anger and disappointment, radical nationalism and fascism began to gain rapid momentum.

In 1919, the poet Gabriele D’Annunzio led a group of nationalists and former Arditi members in the occupation of the Adriatic coastal city of Fiume. As the post-war treaties had not assigned the city to Italy, D’Annunzio resolved to secure victory for the nation through violence—a value he shared with right-wing Futurists such as Marinetti. He established a highly theatrical regime in Fiume, characterised by the widespread use of black uniforms, balcony speeches, torchlight processions, mass cheering and the Roman salute, whilst constantly emphasising nationalism, sacrifice and heroism. These political rituals would later profoundly influence the propaganda methods of fascism.

During the Fiume regime, D’Annunzio also promulgated the Charter of Carnaro, attempting to establish a ‘new state’ that combined nationalism, corporatist economics and strongman politics. As the ‘Comandante’ of the new state, the ‘Reggenza Italiana del Carnaro’, he advocated violence, discipline and the cult of the leader, transforming politics into a ‘performance’ brimming with passion and symbolism. Although the Fiume regime was ultimately suppressed by the Italian government in 1920, it is regarded by many historians as a ‘laboratory’ of fascism, as numerous stylistic elements of the later Italian fascist regime can be traced back to Fiume.

Meanwhile, Benito Mussolini began to organise his own political forces within Italy. In 1919, Mussolini established the “Fasci Italiani di Combattimento” (Italian Combat Fasci) in Milan. This organisation, initially composed of veterans, nationalists, radical youths and anti-socialists, advocated extreme nationalism, anti-communism and the restoration of a powerful state. Mussolini was adept at exploiting the post-war society’s fear of chaos and revolution, presenting fascism as a force capable of restoring order and national honour.

The ‘Blackshirts’ within the Fascist movement subsequently began carrying out violent attacks on socialists, trade unions and left-wing organisations. They set fire to newspaper offices (one of which was L’Avanti!, ironically, a publication for which the young Mussolini had once served as editor-in-chief as a socialist), attacked striking workers, and expanded their influence within local government. As many landowners, industrial capitalists and conservatives feared a communist revolution, they gradually began to support the Fascist movement, hoping to use it to suppress left-wing forces. At the same time, Italy’s liberal government had long been weak and inept, with frequent changes of cabinet in parliament, leading to growing public dissatisfaction with the democratic system.

In October 1922, Mussolini launched the famous ‘March on Rome’. Large numbers of Fascist supporters converged on the capital from across Italy, attempting to force the government to hand over power through political pressure. Although the actual military strength of the Fascist forces was insufficient to truly conquer Rome—of the 100,000 Blackshirts claimed by Mussolini, only 10,000 actually reached the outskirts of the city (by comparison, there were approximately 10,000 elite soldiers from the Royal Army within Rome at the time, fully supplied)—the Fascists had no provisions whatsoever; they broke into nearby farmhouses and seized chickens for their evening meal. However absurd it may have been, this action succeeded in creating a national crisis. The then Prime Minister of Italy, Luigi Facta, demanded that the King declare martial law and deploy the army to suppress the fascists, but King Victor Emmanuel III refused to sign the order.

The King’s hesitation stemmed partly from a fear that suppressing the Fascists would cost him popular support, and partly from a fear that doing so would allow socialist influence to grow too great. Many conservative politicians and senior military figures also believed that Mussolini might be able to restore social order. Consequently, on 29 October 1922, the King invited Mussolini to Rome to form a government. Mussolini thus became the legitimate Prime Minister of Italy, and Fascism formally seized state power.

Benito Mussolini initially continued to govern through a coalition government, but he subsequently began to gradually dismantle Italy’s democratic institutions. To ensure that the Fascist Party could maintain a long-term grip on parliament, Mussolini first amended the electoral system. In 1923, the government passed the notorious Acerbo Law. Under this law, any party securing more than 25 per cent of the vote in a national election would automatically be allocated two-thirds of the seats in parliament. In the general election of 1924, the Fascist Party secured victory through the intimidation and physical assaults of the Blackshirts, as well as electoral fraud, thereby achieving an overwhelming majority in parliament. However, the Socialist MP Giacomo Matteotti publicly denounced the violence and fraud that had marred the election. Shortly afterwards, he was abducted and murdered by Fascists. This incident sent shockwaves throughout the nation and exposed the increasingly violent nature of Fascism. The Matteotti affair plunged Mussolini’s government into crisis. However, as the King, the military and the conservatives remained fearful of a socialist takeover, they ultimately did not overthrow Mussolini. In 1925, Mussolini publicly declared that he would assume ‘political responsibility’ and began to establish a full-fledged dictatorship. Subsequently, opposition parties were banned, press freedom was severely restricted, and local self-government was abolished, transforming Italy into a one-party fascist state.

  

### ADOLF HITLER AND DIE NAZI-PARTEI

>“When the sun of culture is low, even dwarfs cast long shadows.” 
>— Karl Kraus, Austrian Critic

Much like Italy, if not worse, Germany also found itself in a state of severe political and economic turmoil following the end of the First World War. In 1918, the German Empire collapsed, and the Weimar Republic was established following the abdication of Kaiser Wilhelm II. From the outset, this new republic lacked broad social legitimacy. Many Germans believed that the German army had not truly been defeated on the battlefield, but had been betrayed by socialists, republicans and Jews within the country. The Treaty of Versailles imposed massive reparations on Germany, drastically reduced the size of the German military and stripped the country of vast swathes of territory. In the eyes of many Germans, this treaty did not signify peace, but rather a humiliation at the hands of the victorious powers.

Amidst this atmosphere of resentment and anger, politics rapidly began to radicalise. In 1919, Adolf Hitler joined the German Workers’ Party (DAP) in Munich. Thanks to his exceptional oratory skills, Hitler soon rose to prominence within the party. In 1920, the organisation was renamed the National Socialist German Workers’ Party (Nationalsozialistische Deutsche Arbeiterpartei, NSDAP), or the Nazi Party. The Nazi Party combined the ideology of Germanic supremacy, anti-communism, anti-Semitism and a fierce hostility towards the Versailles system, presenting itself as a movement dedicated to restoring Germany’s greatness.

Hitler was deeply influenced by the success of Benito Mussolini and Italian fascism. He particularly admired the drama and awe-inspiring power of the ‘March on Rome’, and believed that Germany could achieve a similar nationalist revolution. Inspired by Mussolini, Hitler launched the famous Beer Hall Putsch in Munich in November 1923 in an attempt to seize power. Together with General Ludendorff and armed members of the Nazi Stormtroopers, he plotted to overthrow the Bavarian government and ultimately march on Berlin. However, unlike Mussolini, Hitler failed to secure sufficient support from the German military, the Junker aristocracy and the capitalist class. The coup quickly collapsed; the Bavarian police opened fire on the marchers in Munich, killing 16 Nazi members. Hitler was subsequently arrested, and the Nazi Party was temporarily banned.

Yet this tactically failed coup ultimately served Hitler strategically. During his trial, he turned the courtroom into a platform for his propaganda, portraying himself as a patriot seeking to save Germany rather than a common criminal. Whilst serving his sentence in Landsberg Prison, he dictated Mein Kampf, a systematic exposition of the ideological foundations of Nazism, including racial hierarchy, the theory of ‘living space’, and the necessity of establishing a dictatorial state.

The Great Depression of 1929 proved to be the decisive turning point in the rise of the Nazi Party. As the American credit system collapsed and German banks failed in droves, millions of Germans were plunged into unemployment and poverty, and an increasing number of the German populace began to lose all faith in the democratic system. The Nazi Party exploited this atmosphere of fear and despair to the full, promising to restore the economy, revitalise the nation and dismantle the Versailles system. They established a vast propaganda machine. Through large-scale rallies, flags, uniforms, radio broadcasts and carefully choreographed mass ceremonies, the Nazis crafted a highly emotional and theatrical form of mass politics that bore a striking resemblance to Italian fascism. Joseph Goebbels, the Nazi Minister of Propaganda, played a pivotal role in shaping Hitler’s image as a ‘savior’. Meanwhile, the SA (Sturmabteilung) engaged in constant violent street clashes with communists and socialists, demonstrating the Nazi Party’s strength on the one hand, whilst further deepening the sense of chaos and crisis within German society on the other.

As the political situation continued to deteriorate, an increasing number of capitalists, the Junker aristocracy and the military began to view Hitler as a tool to prevent a communist revolution. Although few among the traditional conservatives truly subscribed to Nazi ideology, they believed they could control Hitler and utilise the Nazi Party’s mass base to stabilise the national situation. Following a series of political crises, the outcome was a foregone conclusion. On 30 January 1933, President Paul von Hindenburg formally appointed Hitler as Chancellor of Germany.

Upon assuming power, Hitler swiftly dismantled Germany’s democratic institutions. Following the Reichstag fire in February 1933, the Nazis exploited public fears of a communist revolution to launch a massive crackdown on political opposition, resulting in the arrest of thousands of dissidents. Subsequently, the passing of the Enabling Act of 1933 granted the government the power to legislate without parliamentary approval, fundamentally destroying the constitutional foundations of the Weimar Republic.

In the months that followed, opposition parties were banned, trade unions were dissolved, freedom of the press was abolished, and state governments were brought under the complete control of the central government under a policy known as Gleichschaltung (synchronization). In 1934, Hindenburg died. Hitler merged the offices of President and Chancellor, becoming the ‘Führer’ of the German Reich. From then on, Germany became a thoroughly dictatorial state, and fascism was driven in a direction even more radical, racialised and destructive than in Italy.

  

### FEATURES OF FASCIST RULE

>“Si spiritus pro nobis, quis contra nos? (Spirit with us, who against us?)” 
>— Gabriele D’Annunzio

  

Rather than asking what fascism advocates, it is more accurate to ask what it opposes. Early fascism typically combined a reverence for national industrialisation and militarisation, a thirst for war and total national mobilisation, and a pursuit of violence. After Hitler seized power, fascism took on an additional layer of extreme racism. Fascism sometimes espoused anti-communism, yet many of its characteristics bore a striking resemblance to communism; when necessary, German fascism even collaborated with communist Russia. This demonstrates that, although fascism was full of fiery slogans and romantic rhetoric, it was essentially a highly pragmatic political movement that emphasised power and state strength.

Fascism was also a political movement that placed great emphasis on the cult of the leader. In fascist states, the leader is often portrayed as the embodiment of the national will. Benito Mussolini, known as the ‘Duce’; Adolf Hitler, known as the ‘Führer’; and Philippe Pétain, known as the ‘Marshal’—these figures were not merely heads of government, but were portrayed by the propaganda machine as possessing near-divine authority. Fascist regimes constantly reinforced this cult of personality through posters, radio broadcasts, films, mass rallies and school education, causing the people to associate the state, the nation and the leader himself.

At the same time, fascism placed extreme emphasis on mass mobilisation and political rituals. Whether in speeches delivered from the Balcony of the Forum in Rome or the torchlight processions and orderly formations at the Nuremberg Party Congresses, fascist politics was imbued with drama and visual impact. Fascism was not content with the debates and compromises of traditional parliamentary politics, but sought to transform politics into an emotional mass movement. Uniforms, flags, slogans, salutes and collective cheering all became tools for shaping the ‘national community’. Many elements of this political aesthetic can be traced back as far as Gabriele D’Annunzio’s experiments during the Fiume period.

Fascist regimes also generally established highly centralised state apparatuses, using secret police and censorship to suppress dissent. Freedom of the press was abolished, opposition parties were banned, and trade unions and local self-governing bodies were gradually brought under state control. In Germany, the Nazis established the Gestapo and a system of concentration camps; in Italy, anti-fascists were likewise imprisoned, exiled or even assassinated. Although Italian fascist rule was less violent than that of Nazi Germany, both sought to shape a ‘totally unified’ society through fear, propaganda and state control.

However, the most extreme and destructive feature of fascism was ultimately manifested in the racist policies of Nazi Germany. Hitler regarded the ‘Aryan race’ as a superior race and portrayed Jews, Slavs and other minority groups as ‘enemies’ of the German nation. This extreme racism ultimately led to the enactment of the Nuremberg Laws and the systematic extermination of European Jews during the Second World War—known as ‘The Holocaust’. Mussolini, too, had stated in the early days of the Fascist movement: ‘For the revival of Greater Italy, we can exchange the sacrifice of 50,000 Italian soldiers for the deaths of 500,000 Slavic savages.’

Thus, fascism was not merely a form of nationalism or a dictatorial regime; it was, rather, a political phenomenon born of an era of crisis in Europe. Exploiting social fears, economic collapse and a sense of national humiliation, it established totalitarian states through violence, propaganda and mass mobilisation, ultimately plunging Europe into even more catastrophic war and destruction.`
